Ignition System Problems One of the common reasons why a generator may sputter is due to issues within the ignition system. The ignition system is responsible for generating the spark needed to ignite the fuel and start the engine. When this system malfunctions, it can result in erratic engine performance and sputtering. Faulty Ignition Coil An important component of the ignition system is the ignition coil. When the ignition coil is faulty, it can lead to inconsistent electrical output, causing the generator to sputter. If the coil fails to produce a strong spark, the engine may misfire, leading to sputtering and uneven performance. Weak Battery Another factor that can contribute to sputtering is a weak battery. The battery provides the initial electrical power to start the ignition process. Exhaust System Blockage If you notice your generator sputtering and struggling to run smoothly, one possible mechanical issue could be a blockage in the exhaust system. A generator’s exhaust system plays a crucial role in expelling harmful gases and maintaining optimal engine performance. When the exhaust system becomes blocked, it disrupts the natural flow of exhaust gases, causing the generator to sputter and potentially overheat. A common cause of exhaust system blockage is the accumulation of debris, such as leaves, dirt, or small animals’ nests, in the exhaust pipe or muffler. These blockages restrict the flow of exhaust gases, resulting in poor engine performance. To inspect the exhaust system, start by visually checking for any visible blockages. If you detect any debris, use a brush or compressed air to remove it carefully. Be cautious not to damage the exhaust pipe or muffler during the cleaning process.Engine Overheating Another mechanical issue that can cause your generator to sputter is engine overheating. Generators work by converting fuel into mechanical energy, which in turn produces electricity. During this process, the engine generates heat. To prevent overheating, generators are equipped with cooling mechanisms, such as coolant systems and fans. However, if these cooling mechanisms fail or become ineffective, the engine can quickly overheat, leading to sputtering and potential damage. Several factors can contribute to engine overheating, including low coolant levels, a malfunctioning thermostat, or a clogged radiator. A low coolant level prevents the engine from maintaining optimal operating temperatures, while a faulty thermostat fails to regulate the coolant flow properly. Additionally, a clogged radiator obstructs the airflow needed to cool the engine. Regularly inspect and maintain these cooling components to ensure they are functioning correctly. If you suspect engine overheating as the cause of your generator’s sputtering, stop using the generator immediately. Allowing an overheated engine to continue running can lead to severe damage. Begin by checking the coolant levels and topping up if necessary. Inspect the thermostat for any signs of malfunction, such as a stuck valve. Lastly, clean or replace the radiator, ensuring the airflow is unrestricted. Regular Oil Changes One of the most important routine maintenance tasks to keep your generator running smoothly is regular oil changes. Just as in your car, the oil in your generator engine lubricates the moving parts and helps to reduce friction and wear. Over time, the oil can become dirty and contaminated with debris, which can hinder the engine’s performance and cause your generator to sputter. To avoid this, it is recommended to change the oil in your generator every 50 hours of use or at least once a year, whichever comes first. When changing the oil, be sure to use the correct type and grade of oil recommended by the manufacturer. The oil should be poured into the designated oil fill port and checked using the dipstick to ensure it is at the proper level. Regular oil changes not only help to maintain the performance of your generator but also extend its lifespan, reducing the chances of sputtering and other engine issues. Spark Plug Replacement Another crucial maintenance task that can prevent sputtering in your generator is spark plug replacement. The spark plug is responsible for igniting the fuel-air mixture in the engine combustion chamber. Over time, the spark plug can become fouled or worn out, leading to weak or inconsistent sparks, resulting in sputtering or difficulty starting your generator. It is recommended to inspect and replace the spark plug every 100 hours of use or once a year, whichever comes first. To replace the spark plug, first, disconnect the spark plug wire, then use a socket wrench to remove the old spark plug. Take note of the recommended gap specified by the manufacturer and ensure the new spark plug is properly gapped before installing it. By regularly replacing the spark plug, you can ensure a reliable and steady spark, which in turn prevents sputtering and improves the overall performance of your generator. There’s been a lot of chatter lately the Montgomery County disposable bag law, questioning whether the current model is actually reducing litter or just raising money for county coffers. As background, the Montgomery County law (effective 2012) is fairly similar to the one in DC (effective 2010)–both require stores to charge five cents for plastic or paper shopping bags at the point of sale, and remit most of the fee to the local government for environmental improvement projects. DC did extensive research in 2012 and 2013 on the effectiveness of their law, demonstrating that residents were using 60 percent fewer bags than before the law, and that stores were giving out 50 percent fewer bags. The research also showed that the law was extremely popular with both residents and store owners; only 16 percent of residents and 8 percent of retailers opposed it. Data from volunteer stream cleanups in DC have shown a 72 percent decrease in the number of bags removed from streams, parks, and other cleanup sites since the laws took effect. Montgomery County reductions are similar. These reductions haven’t emerged in any other jurisdiction in the region, so it’s likely the bag laws have created this effect. Montgomery County hasn’t done any research on the effectiveness of their law, and councilmembers perceive public opinion to be low. The problem is, policymakers usually only hear from people with complaints; if you’re happy with the status quo, do you feel motivated to call and tell someone that? Probably not. The County law also suffered a public relations setback early on, when revenues came back much higher than expected. We identified the reason why–the estimates were based on a faulty calculation of how many bags residents were using before the law took effect. You can read all the details about that here. Revenue isn’t an accurate measure of the law’s effectiveness anyway. In 5 years, more businesses have opened. New residents have moved to the county. And it’s likely that more businesses are correctly reporting their fee collections now. Of course revenues would go up. There are three key differences between the DC and Montgomery laws, though, that could be holding it back: - The DC law only applies in stores that sell food, while the Montgomery law applies in all stores. The laws are intended to spur behavior change, and people are more familiar with the idea of bringing reusable bags to grocery stores than they are with taking them to the mall. It doesn’t mean it can’t happen, but that particular behavior change needs a lot more educational support than it has received. We successfully defeated an effort to scale back the scope of the law in 2013. - Fees collected in DC go into a specific fund, the Anacostia River Cleanup and Protection Fund. Funds are directed to programs to reduce and remove trash in the Anacostia River as well as programs to support the river’s restoration. The program got some bad press a couple of years ago when a reporter couldn’t see how field trips to teach kids about the environment connected to long-term environmental protection, but in general we know that the public prefers knowing exactly where their funds are being spent. The Montgomery County funds, on the other hand, go into a much larger pot of money, mostly derived from stormwater utility fees. The Water Quality Improvement Fund fulfills the mission of the stormwater fee, paying for rain gardens, pervious pavers, green streets, and other construction projects to reduce polluted runoff into all of the county’s waters. It’s a whole lot harder for residents to see how their bag fees are directly reducing trash pollution. (It doesn’t help that it gets some of the negative connotation of the “rain tax.”) - Finally, DC does extensive outreach and enforcement of the law. District government gives out thousands of free reusable bags, many touting the results of the law. A team of inspectors visits businesses regularly and issues warnings and even citations. Montgomery County has been significantly less proactive with enforcement, preferring a complaint-based approach. They also devote a lot less resources to the outreach effort (though they had this awesome holiday video). They reinvested in the program last summer, and began more proactive enforcement, with the plan of conducting an evaluation of the program in Summer 2017, after a year of the new approach. For that reason, it is still premature to declare the law a failure. It is demonstrably reducing litter in streams, but it could have much more public support if residents and businesses felt it was being implemented fairly and effectively. Would an outright ban be better? Takoma Park just did it, and we advocated for a statewide ban in Maryland in 2015 and 2016. It could be, and if the Montgomery County Council prefers to try that approach, we are ready and able to help. Both models have their benefits, but the Council shouldn’t presume the fee is a failure when their only metrics are squeaky wheels and irregular revenue.
